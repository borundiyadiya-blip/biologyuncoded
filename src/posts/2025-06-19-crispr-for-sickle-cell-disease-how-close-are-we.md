For decades, sickle cell disease has been a painful, life-shortening condition with limited treatment options. But that’s starting to change—thanks to a revolutionary gene-editing technology called **CRISPR**.

In recent years, scientists have made major strides in using CRISPR to treat sickle cell disease at its genetic root. In fact, in 2023, the very first **CRISPR-based therapy** for sickle cell was approved for clinical use in the UK and the US—a historic milestone in medicine.

But what exactly does this mean? And how close are we to making this treatment available to everyone who needs it?

Let’s break it down.

* * *

## What Is Sickle Cell Disease?

Sickle cell disease is a **genetic blood disorder**. People with this condition inherit a faulty gene that affects **haemoglobin**—the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen.

Instead of having flexible, round red blood cells, people with sickle cell disease produce rigid, sickle-shaped cells. These cells:

-   Don’t carry oxygen efficiently
-   Can block blood flow
-   Break apart easily, causing anaemia

This leads to **chronic pain**, **organ damage**, **stroke**, and reduced life expectancy.

There’s no universal cure. Treatments like blood transfusions or pain medication only manage symptoms. Until now.

* * *

## How Can CRISPR Help?

**CRISPR** is a gene-editing tool that allows scientists to **precisely change DNA**. It works like molecular scissors, cutting the DNA at a specific point and allowing scientists to correct, disable, or replace a gene.

For sickle cell disease, researchers are using CRISPR to **target the root cause**: the faulty haemoglobin gene.

But here’s where it gets interesting—most of the successful approaches don’t directly fix the sickle cell gene. Instead, they work around it using a natural backup.

* * *

## The Breakthrough Strategy: Reactivating Foetal Haemoglobin

When we’re born, our bodies naturally produce a type of haemoglobin called **foetal haemoglobin (HbF)**. This version of the protein isn’t affected by the sickle cell mutation—and it actually protects against symptoms of the disease.

In healthy adults, production of foetal haemoglobin is switched off shortly after birth. But people with certain genetic mutations—who continue to produce HbF—often **don’t experience sickle cell symptoms at all**.

CRISPR scientists saw an opportunity: **what if we could use gene editing to turn foetal haemoglobin production back on?**

That’s exactly what a company called **Vertex Pharmaceuticals**, working with **CRISPR Therapeutics**, did with their therapy called **Casgevy**.

* * *

## How Does Casgevy Work?

Casgevy is the first CRISPR-based treatment to receive regulatory approval for sickle cell disease.

Here’s how it works:

1.  **Stem Cells Are Collected**  
    Doctors extract blood stem cells from the patient.
2.  **CRISPR Editing in the Lab**  
    In a lab, scientists use CRISPR to disable a gene called **BCL11A**, which normally suppresses foetal haemoglobin. Once this gene is turned off, the cells start making HbF again.
3.  **Stem Cells Are Returned to the Patient**  
    After a round of chemotherapy to clear out existing bone marrow, the edited stem cells are infused back into the patient.
4.  **The Body Starts Producing Healthy Red Blood Cells**  
    Over time, the new cells take root and begin producing foetal haemoglobin—alleviating or even eliminating symptoms.

* * *

## What Are the Results?

In early clinical trials:

-   Over **90% of patients** treated with this approach became **symptom-free**
-   They no longer needed blood transfusions or hospitalisations for pain crises
-   Many experienced life-changing improvements in daily functioning

These results were strong enough to lead to **regulatory approval in the UK (December 2023)** and **in the US (December 2023)** for patients over 12 years old.

* * *

## What’s the Catch?

While the science is promising, there are still some hurdles:

### 1\. **Complex Treatment Process**

Casgevy is not a simple pill. It requires:

-   Hospital stays
-   Stem cell harvesting
-   High-dose chemotherapy
-   Specialised facilities for gene editing

This makes it **expensive** and **resource-intensive**.

### 2\. **Accessibility**

At the time of approval, only a few hospitals in each country were licensed to deliver the treatment. Widespread access is still being rolled out.

### 3\. **Cost**

Estimates put the cost of treatment at over **$2 million per patient**. While this may still be cost-effective compared to lifelong care, it raises big questions about **insurance coverage and global equity**.

* * *

## So, How Close Are We?

The science is here. The first approvals have happened. But **accessibility, cost, and scale** are the next big challenges.

In short:

-   For patients in high-income countries with access to cutting-edge care, this treatment is already becoming a reality.
-   For the rest of the world—especially in places like sub-Saharan Africa, where sickle cell is most common—there’s still a long way to go.

But even so, we’ve reached a turning point. For the first time, we can talk about **curing** a genetic disease by rewriting the DNA in a patient’s own cells.

* * *

## Final Thought

CRISPR isn’t just a cool lab tool anymore—it’s a real medical therapy changing real lives.

Sickle cell disease has long been one of the clearest targets for gene editing. And now, we’re seeing what was once a dream become clinical reality.

The next challenge is making sure that promise reaches **every patient**, not just a few.
