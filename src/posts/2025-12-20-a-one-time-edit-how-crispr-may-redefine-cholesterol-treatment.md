For decades, managing high cholesterol has meant lifelong adherence to daily pills or periodic injections—an effective but imperfect strategy in a world where medication adherence steadily declines over time. New findings unveiled at the American Heart Association’s Scientific Sessions 2025 suggest a radically different future: one where a single gene-editing treatment may durably lower cholesterol and triglycerides at their genetic source.

In a first-in-human Phase 1 trial, researchers tested an investigational CRISPR-Cas9 therapy, CTX310™, designed to switch off the _ANGPTL3_ gene in liver cells. This gene plays a central role in regulating blood lipid levels, and individuals born with naturally inactive _ANGPTL3_ mutations exhibit lifelong low cholesterol and reduced cardiovascular risk without apparent adverse effects. Rather than mimicking this biology with drugs, CTX310 aims to recreate it directly in DNA.

The results were striking. A one-time intravenous infusion reduced LDL (“bad”) cholesterol by nearly 50% and triglycerides by about 55%, with some participants seeing reductions as high as 60% at the highest dose. These effects emerged within two weeks and persisted throughout the 60-day follow-up period, exceeding expectations for an early-stage safety trial. Notably, this is the first therapy shown to simultaneously produce large reductions in both LDL cholesterol and triglycerides—a longstanding challenge for patients with mixed lipid disorders.

Safety, the primary goal of a Phase 1 trial, was also encouraging. Most side effects were mild and infusion-related, such as nausea or back pain, and resolved quickly with standard treatment. One participant experienced a temporary rise in liver enzymes that normalized without intervention. No serious or long-term safety concerns have been identified so far, though participants will continue to be monitored for years, in line with FDA guidance for CRISPR-based therapies.

Beyond the numbers, the implications are profound. High cholesterol remains one of the most significant modifiable risk factors for heart disease and stroke, affecting tens of millions of adults in the United States alone. While existing therapies are effective, real-world outcomes are limited by cost, access, and adherence. A durable, one-and-done genetic intervention could shift cardiovascular care from chronic management to long-term prevention, particularly for patients with inherited or treatment-resistant lipid disorders.

At the same time, this study represents a beginning rather than a conclusion. The trial involved just 15 participants, most of whom were male and based in Australia, New Zealand, and the UK. Larger and more diverse Phase 2 studies, expected to begin soon, will be essential to confirm efficacy, assess long-term safety, and determine who stands to benefit most.

Still, CTX310 offers a glimpse into a future where cardiovascular disease is tackled not just with better drugs, but with precise molecular rewrites. If subsequent trials confirm these early results, CRISPR gene editing may become a cornerstone of preventive cardiology—editing risk out of existence before disease has a chance to take hold.
