CRISPR/Cas technology has revolutionized biology, allowing scientists to edit genes with unprecedented precision. But even the most sophisticated gene-editing tools are only as effective as the systems used to deliver them into cells. Over the past decade, researchers have developed a variety of innovative delivery mechanisms, each with unique advantages and challenges. From viral vectors to polymer nanoparticles, let’s dive into the cutting-edge strategies shaping the future of gene therapy.

* * *

## 1\. Viral Delivery Systems

Viral vectors are naturally evolved to deliver genetic material into cells, making them highly efficient carriers for CRISPR/Cas systems. Among the most commonly used viral vectors are **Adeno-Associated Virus (AAV)**, **Adenovirus (AV)**, and **Lentivirus (LV)**.

-   **Adeno-Associated Virus (AAV)**
    -   **Advantages**: Non-pathogenic, low immunogenicity, capable of long-term expression in non-dividing cells.
    -   **Applications**: Widely used for in vivo gene therapy, including targeting the retina, liver, and muscle (DMD). AAVs are particularly effective in diseases like Leber congenital amaurosis and hemophilia.
    -   **Limitations**: Small packaging capacity (~4.7 kb) limits delivery of large CRISPR/Cas constructs; pre-existing immunity in patients can reduce efficiency.
-   **Adenovirus (AV)**
    -   **Advantages**: High transduction efficiency in dividing and non-dividing cells, large packaging capacity (~8 kb), and strong gene expression.
    -   **Applications**: Used in ex vivo CRISPR editing of immune cells or tumor cells for cancer therapies. AV vectors can deliver both Cas9 and guide RNAs efficiently.
    -   **Limitations**: High immunogenicity can trigger inflammation and reduce safety for systemic delivery.
-   **Lentivirus (LV)**
    -   **Advantages**: Integrates into the host genome for stable long-term expression, can infect dividing and non-dividing cells, large payload capacity (~8–10 kb).
    -   **Applications**: Commonly used for ex vivo gene editing in stem cells, CAR-T cell therapies, and other hematopoietic cell modifications.
    -   **Limitations**: Risk of insertional mutagenesis due to genomic integration; careful vector design is needed to enhance safety.

Viral vectors remain some of the most **efficient and well-characterized delivery methods**, especially in clinical and ex vivo applications, although safety and immunogenicity concerns have spurred interest in non-viral alternatives.

* * *

## 2\. Polymer Nanoparticles (PNPs)

Polymer nanoparticles are versatile carriers that can package CRISPR/Cas9 systems for precise gene editing. These nanoparticles are usually formed through **electrostatic interactions** between cationic polymers and nucleic acids, condensing the genetic cargo into compact structures.

-   **Advantages**:
    -   Protect nucleic acids from degradation.
    -   Extend circulation time in the blood.
    -   Enable **active targeting** through surface modifications.
-   **Innovations**:
    -   **Fluorinated polyethyleneimine (PF33)** achieved ~90% transfection efficiency in tumor cells.
    -   **Core–shell PNPs** coated with hyaluronic acid allow tumor-specific targeting and lysosomal escape via the proton sponge effect.
    -   **Semiconducting PNPs** enable spatiotemporal control of gene editing using light or ultrasound.
    -   **Responsive PNPs** react to pH, glutathione (GSH), or reactive oxygen species (ROS) for controlled release.

PNPs not only deliver CRISPR machinery but can also combine gene editing with conventional drug therapies for enhanced treatment efficacy.

* * *

## 3\. Biomimetic Nanoparticles

Biomimetic nanoparticles borrow strategies from nature to improve biocompatibility and targeting. They often use **cell membranes (CM)** from cancer cells, immune cells, or stem cells to coat the nanoparticles.

-   **Advantages**: Reduced immunogenicity, prolonged circulation, and specific targeting.
-   **Examples**: Cancer cell membranes coated on Cas9 facilitate tumor targeting; MSC membranes can help edit immune-related genes like IL1RAP; ONCOTECH combines CAR-T cells and CRISPR/Cas9 to enhance tumor recognition.

Biomimetic nanoparticles leverage natural targeting mechanisms, reducing side effects and increasing delivery efficiency.

* * *

## 4\. Extracellular Vesicles (EVs)

EVs are naturally produced vesicles that transport proteins, RNA, and other molecules between cells.

-   **Advantages**: High stability in biofluids, long-distance transport without immune clearance, and modifiable for targeted delivery.
-   **Applications**: Exosomes engineered to deliver CRISPRi to tumor-associated macrophages, Cas9 RNP delivery for liver therapies, and aptamer-conjugated EVs for hard-to-reach cells.

EVs combine natural targeting with the ability to carry both RNA and protein forms of CRISPR.

* * *

## 5\. Inorganic Nanoparticles

Inorganic nanoparticles, like gold (Au), mesoporous silica (MSNs), and iron oxide (Fe3O4), offer **physical properties** that make them ideal for combined gene and photothermal therapies.

-   **Gold nanoparticles** deliver CRISPR/Cas9 while generating immunogenic cell death under near-infrared light.
-   **MSNs** allow co-delivery of CRISPR and small molecules.
-   **Magnetic nanoparticles** enable field-controlled gene editing.

These nanoparticles often serve dual purposes: gene editing and therapy enhancement via photothermal or magnetic control.

* * *

## 6\. Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs)

MOFs are hybrid materials combining metals and organic ligands.

-   **Applications**: ZIF-8 MOFs deliver Cas9 RNP and inhibitors for tumor metabolism control; cell membrane-coated MOFs enhance targeting; acid-responsive MOFs allow controlled gene editing for immunotherapy or bone diseases.

MOFs provide multifunctional, environmentally responsive CRISPR delivery platforms.

* * *

## 7\. Virus-Like Particles (VLPs) and Extracellular Contractile Injection Systems (eCIS)

-   **VLPs**: Non-replicating viral mimics that package Cas9 RNP or base editors for precise editing. Safe, highly efficient, and suitable for in vivo therapy.
-   **eCIS**: Phage tail-like structures capable of programmable protein injection. Nearly 100% delivery efficiency without triggering inflammation.

These platforms merge viral efficiency with non-viral safety.

* * *

## 8\. Conclusion

The past decade has seen explosive innovation in **CRISPR delivery mechanisms**. From **viral vectors** like AAV, AV, and LV, to synthetic polymers, biomimetic materials, EVs, inorganic nanoparticles, MOFs, and virus-inspired systems, researchers now have a versatile toolkit for gene therapy.

-   **Key trends**:
    -   Targeted, controlled delivery to minimize off-target effects.
    -   Integration with **imaging, photothermal, and immunomodulatory therapies**.
    -   Exploration of **nature-inspired strategies** for biocompatibility.

While viral vectors remain highly efficient, non-viral platforms offer safer and more flexible alternatives. The combination of **precision, safety, and versatility** will ultimately determine which delivery strategies transform CRISPR from an experimental tool into a standard therapy for human diseases.
