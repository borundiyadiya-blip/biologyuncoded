Most people think of vaccines as tools to prevent infections like measles or the flu. But a new kind of vaccine is emerging—one that doesn’t stop a virus, but helps the immune system find and destroy **cancer**.

These are known as **cancer vaccines**, and while the field is still developing, early results are promising. Some vaccines aim to treat cancers that already exist, while others aim to prevent cancers linked to viruses. The idea is simple but powerful: can we **teach the immune system** to recognise cancer the way it does an infection?

In this post, we’ll break down what cancer vaccines are, what types exist, how they work, and where the research stands today.

* * *

## What Are Cancer Vaccines?

There are two main categories of cancer vaccines:

### 1\. **Preventive Cancer Vaccines**

These are designed to prevent cancer—but they do so **indirectly**, by targeting viruses that can cause cancer later on.

So far, there are only two approved vaccines in this category:

-   **HPV Vaccine (e.g. Gardasil)**: Prevents infection with human papillomavirus, which can cause cervical, throat, anal, and other cancers.
-   **Hepatitis B Vaccine**: Prevents hepatitis B infection, which increases the risk of liver cancer.

These vaccines don’t target cancer cells directly. Instead, they block the **viral infections** that raise the risk of cancer developing in the first place.

There are **no vaccines yet** that prevent cancers not caused by viruses—like breast, lung, or colon cancer. But researchers are working on it.

### 2\. **Therapeutic Cancer Vaccines**

These are designed to treat existing cancers by **stimulating the immune system** to recognise and attack cancer cells.

Unlike preventive vaccines, these don’t stop cancer from starting. Instead, they aim to **slow tumour growth**, prevent recurrence, or even shrink existing cancers.

This is where most current cancer vaccine research is focused.

* * *

## How Do Therapeutic Cancer Vaccines Work?

The immune system is constantly patrolling the body, looking for anything abnormal. In theory, it should be able to spot cancer cells. But tumours are tricky—they can disguise themselves or suppress immune responses.

Cancer vaccines aim to:

-   Expose the immune system to **antigens** (proteins found on cancer cells)
-   Trigger an immune response that **targets those antigens**
-   Help the body remember how to attack cancer if it comes back

Some therapeutic vaccines are **off-the-shelf**, targeting common markers found on many people’s tumours. Others are **personalised**, made from a patient’s own tumour cells to train the immune system with highly specific targets.

* * *

## Examples of Cancer Vaccine Research

### **Melanoma**

Several vaccines have shown success in training the immune system to attack melanoma cells, especially when combined with other treatments like checkpoint inhibitors.

### **Pancreatic and Lung Cancer**

mRNA vaccines—using the same technology as some COVID-19 vaccines—are being tested to stimulate immune responses in hard-to-treat cancers like pancreatic and non-small cell lung cancer.

### **Cervical and Head & Neck Cancers**

Because these are often linked to HPV, researchers are testing therapeutic vaccines that target HPV-infected tumour cells, even after cancer has formed.

### **BioNTech’s Personalised mRNA Vaccine**

In one of the most promising trials, researchers created **individualised vaccines** for melanoma patients by analysing each tumour’s mutations and designing a custom mRNA shot. The result: reduced risk of cancer coming back after surgery.

* * *

## What Makes Cancer Vaccines Promising?

-   **Precision**: They train the immune system to attack only cancer cells.
-   **Fewer Side Effects**: Compared to chemotherapy or radiation, vaccines are often better tolerated.
-   **Immune Memory**: Once trained, the immune system may stay vigilant long after treatment ends.
-   **Combining Therapies**: Vaccines can boost the effectiveness of existing immunotherapies.

* * *

## What Are the Challenges?

-   **Cancer’s Complexity**: Every tumour is different. Creating a vaccine that works across patients is tough.
-   **Immune Evasion**: Some tumours actively suppress the immune system, making vaccines less effective.
-   **Early Trials**: Most cancer vaccines are still experimental, with only a few in late-stage testing.
-   **Cost and Time**: Personalised vaccines are expensive and slow to produce, though mRNA tech may speed things up.

* * *

## Are Any Cancer Vaccines Approved to Treat Cancer?

Only one therapeutic cancer vaccine has received FDA approval so far:

-   **Sipuleucel-T (Provenge)** for advanced prostate cancer. It uses the patient’s own immune cells, trained in a lab to attack prostate cancer cells. The benefit is modest, but it proved the concept.

More therapeutic vaccines are in clinical trials and could be approved in the next few years.

* * *

## Final Thought

Cancer vaccines aren’t science fiction—they’re here, and they’re evolving fast. While no single vaccine can cure all cancers, the progress being made—especially with mRNA platforms and personalised medicine—is impressive.

Preventive vaccines like those for HPV and hepatitis B have already changed the landscape of cancer prevention. Now, therapeutic vaccines are opening the door to a future where we don’t just treat cancer—we teach the body to **fight it more intelligently**.

It’s not the end of cancer, but it could be the beginning of **smarter, more targeted, and longer-lasting treatment**.
