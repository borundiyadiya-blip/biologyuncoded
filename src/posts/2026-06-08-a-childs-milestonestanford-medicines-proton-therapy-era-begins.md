On June 4, 2025, a seven-year-old with a rare brain tumor made history — becoming the first patient ever treated at Stanford Medicine’s new proton therapy center.

There are moments in medicine that feel quietly extraordinary — not headline-grabbing, but deeply human. A seven-year-old child, facing a diagnosis no family should ever have to hear, walked into Stanford Medicine’s new proton therapy facility on June 4 and became part of history.

The child, diagnosed with a rare brain tumor, was the first patient to receive treatment at the newly opened Stanford Medicine proton therapy center — a facility years in the making and representing a significant leap forward for precision cancer care on the West Coast.

Proton therapy is not a new concept, but access to it has long been limited. Unlike conventional X-ray radiation, which deposits energy both before and after reaching a tumor, protons can be tuned to release the bulk of their energy at a precise depth — a phenomenon known as the Bragg peak. For tumors nestled near critical structures like the brainstem, optic nerves, or spinal cord, this precision is not just a technical advantage. It can mean the difference between a child who grows up with lasting side effects and one who doesn’t.

“When you’re treating a seven-year-old, you’re not just thinking about the tumor. You’re thinking about the next seventy years of their life.”

Pediatric brain tumors occupy a particularly difficult corner of oncology. Children’s developing brains are exquisitely sensitive to radiation — conventional treatment can affect cognition, growth, and hormonal development for years, sometimes decades. The case for proton therapy in young patients has been building for years in the research literature, and Stanford’s new facility is now positioned to offer it closer to home for patients in Northern California and beyond.

The opening of Stanford Medicine’s proton therapy center marks the culmination of significant investment and planning. Proton therapy machines — called cyclotrons or synchrotrons depending on their design — are enormous, expensive, and technically demanding to operate. A single treatment room requires shielding measured in feet of concrete. Building a center requires not just capital but an entire ecosystem of medical physicists, radiation oncologists, and specialized nurses working in concert.

For the family of the seven-year-old who became patient number one, the medical details may matter less than a simpler truth: their child received cutting-edge care close to home, at a center built precisely for cases like theirs. That, too, is part of what a milestone looks like.

Stanford Medicine has said the facility is now open for patients, with referrals being accepted for a range of cancers where proton therapy offers meaningful advantages — including pediatric tumors, skull base tumors, and cancers near the spine. As the center ramps up operations, the hope is that the first patient’s story is one of many: children and adults given a more precise path through treatment and toward life on the other side of it.
