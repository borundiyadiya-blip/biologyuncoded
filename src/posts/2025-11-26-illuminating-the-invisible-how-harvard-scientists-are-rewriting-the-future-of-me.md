## **Turning Bacterial Evolution Against Itself**

Antibiotic resistance remains one of the deadliest biological crises on Earth, responsible for more than **1.3 million deaths each year**. Yet the threat is not driven solely by the bacteria themselves, but by the tiny genetic elements they carry: **plasmids**. These self-replicating DNA circles roam freely inside bacterial cells, spreading resistance genes with alarming efficiency.

In a new _Science_ paper published November 20, researchers in the labs of **Michael Baym** and **Johan Paulsson** at the Blavatnik Institute at Harvard Medical School devised an ingenious system to watch plasmid evolution unfold in real time. Their aim was simple yet unprecedented: to understand how plasmids compete, mutate, and rise to dominance inside a single bacterial cell.

To achieve this, the team, led by first author **Fernando Rossine**, solved two long-standing technical barriers. First, they engineered bacterial populations so that every cell began with an equal balance of two plasmids — a controlled evolutionary battleground. Then they used microfluidic devices to isolate single cells, ensuring that the patterns they observed came solely from **intracellular plasmid competition**, not environmental noise.

This controlled evolutionary arena allowed the researchers to map new principles of how plasmids gain or lose fitness. They uncovered constraints that shape plasmid evolution — constraints that could, in the future, be weaponized. If scientists can interfere with the very rules that allow plasmids to “learn” resistance, they may be able to prevent bacteria from outsmarting antibiotics in the first place.

Rossine puts it succinctly: the work provides “**new tools to fight and prevent antibiotic resistance by weaponizing the intracellular competition between mobile genetic elements themselves.**” Beyond its practical applications, the study offers a philosophical reminder: evolution is not a single, linear story. It unfolds simultaneously across competing levels — genes, plasmids, cells, and populations — each shaping the fate of life’s smallest battles.

## **A New Dimension in Human Tissue Imaging**

While one team is rewriting our understanding of microbial evolution, another is redefining how we see human tissues themselves.

Scientists in the lab of **Peter Sorger**, the Otto Krayer Professor of Systems Pharmacology at Harvard Medical School, have developed a technique called **cyclic immunofluorescence (CyCIF)** that transforms ordinary tissue samples into intricate **3D cellular landscapes**. Their results, recently published in _Nature Methods_, showcase an imaging approach that reveals structures never before visible: the nuanced shapes of immune cells, the architecture of blood vessels, and the spatial conversations between tumor cells and their neighbors.

Traditional pathology relies heavily on thin 2D slices of tissue. CyCIF overturns this limitation. By repeatedly staining, imaging, and stripping markers from the same sample, researchers can stack information layer by layer, ultimately reconstructing a full volumetric model of the tissue. This reveals cellular interactions that are essential for understanding not just what diseases look like, but how they behave.

For cancer research, this is transformative. Tumors are more than collections of malignant cells; they are ecosystems. CyCIF lets scientists track how immune cells infiltrate tumors, how cancer cells shape their microenvironment, and where therapeutic targets might hide in the dense tissue architecture. This three-dimensional clarity may inspire new ideas for drug development and help unlock therapies that target diseases with unprecedented precision.

## **Looking Ahead**

Both of these advances — manipulating evolutionary dynamics inside bacteria and mapping human tissues in 3D — demonstrate the power of scientific creativity. They also underscore a larger truth: medicine’s future will be shaped not just by new technologies, but by new ways of seeing and understanding life itself.

As we give thanks this week, we celebrate the researchers who push boundaries and embrace the unknown. Their work illuminates the invisible, challenges assumptions, and brings us closer to a world where antibiotic resistance is no longer a global catastrophe and cancer can be understood cell by cell, layer by layer.

The pace of biomedical innovation continues because of the dedication of this community. And with discoveries like these, the future of medicine grows brighter — and more imaginative — every day.
