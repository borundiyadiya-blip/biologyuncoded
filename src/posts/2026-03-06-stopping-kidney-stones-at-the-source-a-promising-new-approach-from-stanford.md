Anyone who has ever had a kidney stone knows the drill: excruciating pain (often compared to childbirth), emergency room visits, and the looming anxiety that another one could strike at any moment. Affecting roughly 1 in 10 people, kidney stones are a remarkably common, yet notoriously stubborn, problem to solve.

But there’s good news on the horizon. Dr. Alan Pao, director of the Stanford Medicine Kidney Stone Clinic, is leading research that could finally offer a reliable, targeted way to prevent recurrent stones.

* * *

### The Problem with Current Treatments

If you suffer from recurrent kidney stones, your options for prevention are currently limited. Doctors typically suggest dietary tweaks or prescribe generic medications, but these aren’t always effective—and they can come with frustrating side effects.

-   **The Citrate Shortage:** Up to half of kidney stone patients have unusually low levels of **citrate** in their urine. Citrate is a naturally occurring chemical that is crucial for prevention because it binds to calcium, keeping it dissolved so it can be urinated out harmlessly instead of forming hard deposits.
-   **The pH Dilemma:** While some existing drugs can boost citrate levels, they also raise the pH of the urine. Unfortunately, higher urine pH can promote the formation of entirely _different_ types of stones. This catch-22 is particularly problematic for women, who are more likely to experience the tricky combination of low citrate and high urine pH.

### A Molecular Pump Holds the Key

Dr. Pao and his team are bypassing this pH problem by focusing on a specific protein in the kidney cells called **NaDC1**.

Think of NaDC1 as a molecular pump. Its job is to pull citrate out of the urine and reabsorb it back into the kidneys. When NaDC1 is overactive, it takes away too much citrate, leaving calcium free to crystallize into stones.

By developing a drug that directly blocks this NaDC1 pump, Dr. Pao aims to keep citrate levels high in the urine without altering the overall pH balance—effectively eliminating the side effects that limit today’s medications.

### From the Lab to the Clinic

Working alongside the Stanford Innovative Medicines Accelerator, the research team is making significant strides:

-   **Lab Success:** They have successfully identified two classes of compounds that effectively block the NaDC1 protein in lab-grown cells.
-   **Next Steps:** The team is currently testing these derivative compounds in mouse models. The goal is to find a formulation that safely reaches the urine while maintaining its blocking effects. If successful, this will pave the way for human clinical trials.

### Driven by Patient Experiences

For Dr. Pao, the inspiration comes directly from his clinic. His patients are desperate for personalized, effective solutions rather than the generic informational pamphlets they often receive.

Take Garrett Farwell, a software sales team member who ended up in the ER on his 39th birthday with a surprise kidney stone. Inspired by Dr. Pao’s targeted approach, Farwell has actually become a financial supporter of the research. As he notes, a new drug wouldn’t just bring physical relief; it would alleviate the constant psychological anxiety of waiting for the next stone to strike.

As Dr. Pao puts it, “My clinical work recharges my basic science work; it reminds me why we do the science.”
